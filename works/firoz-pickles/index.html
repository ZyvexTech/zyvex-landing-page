The brief

Firoz Pickles came to us with a Shopify store and steady organic traffic that was not turning into orders. We fixed the store first: conversion rate, checkout and average order value, with no ad spend. Only once the store converted did we switch on Meta Ads. Paid traffic now lands on a funnel built to close it, which is why the returns below are possible.

Meta Ads · 12 October 2026

One Day, Both Sides of the Ledger

A normal day on the account, shown in full. On 12 October the campaign returned 10.36x its ad spend on 19 website purchases, at a 5.34% click-through rate. Shopify recorded 29 orders from 600 sessions that day, with sales up 80% and sessions up 44% on the day before. Shopify counts every channel, organic and SEO included, so its order count runs above the purchases Meta attributes to ads.

Peak day

46x Return on a Single Day

The best day on the account so far returned 46.23x its ad spend, with four purchases from just 24 link clicks. To be clear, that is one day, not the average. Daily ROAS has typically run between 9x and 15x, reaching 18x on strong days. We show the peak because it shows how efficiently a store that already converts can turn small amounts of paid traffic into orders.

What comes next

Scaling Operations Before Spend

ROAS at this level is the signal to scale, but raising the budget before the business can deliver turns good returns into late orders and refunds. So the next phase starts behind the scenes. Production and delivery teams are being set up to handle higher volume, and the website, built some time ago, is being redesigned for the next stage of growth. Ad spend rises once both are ready.

Before the ads

The Groundwork: A Store That Converts

The ad results rest on CRO work done first, with no paid traffic at all. Here is how that went.

01

Traffic Was Never the Problem

We started with a funnel and store CRO audit to find where visitors were actually dropping off, rather than guessing from top-line analytics. The audit is the whole engagement in miniature: find the leak before prescribing the fix.

02

Fixing the Two Stages That Cost the Most

Work was prioritized on the landing page and the checkout, the two stages where small friction costs the most completed orders. Changes were structured as testable improvements, so a result could be attributed to a specific fix rather than to a general redesign.

03

Raising Average Order Value

Conversion rate alone only gets you part of the way. We worked on average order value in parallel, so that each converted visitor was worth more. Every product page got a Buy More quantity selector, with the three-jar pack flagged as Best Selling, plus an order-by countdown that shows the delivery window. Buying more than one jar becomes the default choice, not an afterthought. That is the second multiplier that turns a conversion lift into a sales lift.

04

2.4x Average Conversion, 3x Average Sales

On average, conversion rate came out 2.4x higher. With average order value lifted alongside it, total sales averaged 3x, on the same organic traffic the store already had. The two numbers measure different things and multiply together, which is why the sales figure is higher than the conversion figure. Strong days ran well above the average: on 26 August the store converted at 15%, up 530%, with sales up 242% and orders up 200%.

The outcome

First, the store: average conversion rate up 2.4x and average sales up 3x on the same organic traffic, with no ad spend. Then, the ads: Meta campaigns running at 9 to 15x ROAS on most days, peaking at 46x. Next comes scaling, done in order: operations and the new website first, bigger budgets second.
